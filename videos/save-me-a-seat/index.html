How it was drawn

There is no AI video in it, and no 3D. Every frame of Save Me a Seat is drawn by code on a flat canvas, the way the people who post “I animated this myself” videos draw theirs by hand: one cartoon puppet with rubber-hose arms, a few hundred props, and fifty-six shots cut on the song’s beats.

The ink is a tapered line that wobbles a little differently on every drawing. The colour is watercolour that sits a touch off its line and pools at its edges, and the paper itself changes sheet with every drawing. It is animated on twos, twelve drawings a second, so the lines boil the way hand-drawn lines do.

The song’s pictures are taken literally. The stars really are stacked like folding chairs. The moon is a bulb on a pull chain, and the end of time is the end of the page: a hill of folding chairs that stops at a torn edge, with nothing beyond it but the dark.

The memories are crayoned in inside a hand-ruled frame, and the bridge drops to bare pencil, until the page folds itself up and every folded star rushes into one point of light. That light becomes a doorway, and she comes through it, at last, running a little behind.

One colour

Her yellow raincoat is the only strong colour in the film. Everything else is ink, paper, and night.

Never her face

The girl in the raincoat stays in the shadow of her hood, with red curls spilling out. We never see her clearly.

Held in a hand

Every phone is held by somebody’s hand, right up to the last one, ringing in a pocket.

No words on screen

The lyrics never appear in the picture. Each line gets a shot that shows what it means instead.
