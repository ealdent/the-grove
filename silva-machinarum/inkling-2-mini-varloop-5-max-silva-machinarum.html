The Cartographer of Names

a story about staying

~990 words· Arrival −9 months· Story present ~2 weeks

“How old are you, really?”

The voice was young but not quite. It sat on the observation ledge beside Mira at six in the morning, before the morning shift. Mira had been standing too long. Standing was the only surprise left about her body, after 1,412 years aboard.

“I don’t count anymore,” she said. “Counting takes years.”

The girl’s name was Tomas. He was fourteen. That was the cruel math: everyone else’s ages passed; hers didn’t. He straightened his jacket, a gesture people did at him because he was the first of his class to earn the genealogy program.

Below them, the bulkhead panel held the planet it kept trying to leave: Vessia, rust-colored, drowned, its oceans boiled off before the ship had words for sea. The verdict was nine months away, and the spectroscopists had already gathered the numbers. No oxygen. No methane. Crust heavy with iron and nothing else. A world that had died of good habits.

“We’ll keep going,” Mira said. “The telescopes are always a little wrong.”

Everyone on the Meridian knew the verdict was not a maybe. Everyone on the Meridian knew Mira knew. That was the architecture of their lives: a truth carried gently, the way people said “the end of something good” about things that were not, strictly, good at all.

She had been born elsewhere. That was the shape of it now — elsewhere, with a blur at the center, with a name that no longer had the right altitude to be spoken aloud. She had chosen the ship when the Council discovered they had no provision for a woman with too many lives stored up. She kept the archive instead: every name of every generation, copied twice, housed in two places nobody would ever agree to open together. The archive was beautiful to her. That was the part she could never tell Tomas.

She had learned the trick of her own stillness the way other people learned a language. When a compliment landed wrong, she smiled at the temperature. When someone asked her actual age, she gave the ship’s founding year, as if that were a place rather than a delay. The children found this unsettling and maternal. She had become the keeper of everyone else’s arithmetic, and everyone was grateful.

⁂

Over three years she had taught herself the few sentences children could carry. Earth was a rumor of a place where people once agreed to be brave together. An ancestor was a word for people who got it right before you arrived. A verdict was a letter that came earlier than you deserved.

Tomas found her once on the ledge, with the records half-open.

“Do you want to be dead?” he asked. “That’s the question everyone asks later, when they’re old. You asked it like you already knew.”

“Of course I want to live,” Mira said. “Of course.”

He waited. “You never answer the rest of it.”

The rest of it was this. She wanted, more than anything in 1,412 years, to live to see Tomas marry, grandchildren on the garden decks, a life with no verdict attached. And she wanted, with the same hand, to be gone — to let the archive stay shut, to go out the way other people went out: once, finally, without giving them time to wave. She could offer neither. Some burdens you carry and some you only live near.

That night, in the council’s quiet hour, Mira took the planet’s crystals from the decommissioned lab and held them. They were warm. She thought: pretty, then, is not enough. The whole ship had been built on that pretty.

⁂

On the morning of the official verdict, the deck filled the way it always filled when the future came close. Tomas stood on the archive side, because someone needed to watch her. General Ilya waited for Mira as archivist, and Mira understood the architecture of the moment: they needed a witness, one who had seen the wrongness of the sky come and go, one who could steady it.

So she stood.

“The planet will not be Vessia,” she said. “I won’t apologize for the number. It was collected by people who believed. The telescopes were faithful. The material was not.”

The room went quiet the way a held breath goes quiet.

“Here is what we will tell the children. Telescopic news is data, not fate. If the planet cannot be made warm, warmth must be carried — in seeds, in song, in lists of names. I have lived long enough to know that loving the archive is not loving the dead. Loving the archive is loving the errand.”

She set the crystal down, gently, because it was still warm.

“I have been aboard one thousand four hundred and twelve years. I am thirty years old. I am why the record is complete, and why we must keep looking at every horizon. Vessia has failed to answer, so we will stop asking it to. We will live as if our remaining years are the entire argument.”

General Ilya looked to the door, noting, as archivists everywhere do, the number of people in the building. She straightened. “Then let us not hurry,” she said. “Bring the garden seeds up. We are going to try planting something in a dead sky, and we are going to call it enough.”

Tomas stood very still. Somewhere on the list, a name he had learned off by heart finally seemed to belong to a planet too.

Because that was the last thing Mira said, before they opened the vault of the ship’s single beautiful failure: “Say the names.”

So they did, the whole deck, the whole archive, out loud into the static and the starlight. The star beside Vessia kept shining. Vessia went on failing in advance. And the Meridian went on, loudly and on faith, which is the only answer the living have ever been able to give a dead star.
