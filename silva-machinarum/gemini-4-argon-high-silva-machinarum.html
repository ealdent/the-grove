Drum One · Ship Year 238

The Ambering

Glass darkens between the stars at the same rate as a mortal eye, which is why I have spent five lifetimes aboard the Samara grinding my own old age by hand.

In Drum One’s optics shop, where I polished lenses as Sera, I kept a locked tray of corneal shells. Everyone inside our spinning cylinders gathers cosmic radiation through the hull: by forty, natural lenses tint straw; by seventy, they turn the smoky honey clerks call the ambering. Because bulkhead gates scan retinal amber at every census, a woman whose cells repair radiation overnight cannot pass a hatch with young eyes.

Each January for fifty-four years, I ground cerium-doped hydrogel shells, darkened them under an ultraviolet lamp, and etched counterfeit capillaries across the curve. Every morning I slipped them over my corneas, creased my cheeks with walnut stain and alum, and wore lead shims in my heels to stoop.

On the rolls I was eighty-one. Tomorrow night I would load a shroud with sixty-one kilograms of cullet and pitch, fool the Solar Sluice reader with my eighty-year-old lenses, and vent my weighted double into the wake. Then I would crush the amber shells, break my nose with a mallet so it set crooked, and climb to Drum Three as twenty-three-year-old Nora.

It would be my sixth life aboard, my ninetieth since Ugarit. I had embarked two hundred and thirty-eight years ago not for a promised world, but because Earth was dying, and I feared being the last thing breathing on a cinder. I needed mortals like loam needs rain.

Then Anya knocked at my shutter, bringing the smell of cold iron.

At twenty-six, Anya was Chief Spectroscopist at the Long Glass, whose mirror I had ground two lives ago. She was also my great-granddaughter—not by blood, for my womb had been barren since the Bronze Age, but by the only love that ever broke my caution.

“The dust cleared off Pelagos,” Anya said, setting her spectrographic plates on my bench. “There is no biosphere, Sera. The oxygen is abiotic.”

I poured hot chicory with hands I forced to shake. “We saw water bands.”

“Bleach and vinegar,” she whispered. “Pelagos orbits inside its star’s flare belt. Ultraviolet storms have split its water, stripping hydrogen to space and baking the plains into perchlorate rust. Anything from the vaults will blister in an hour.” She pressed her forehead to my knuckles. “At midnight the Council votes to abort landing, mulch the apple grafts, and seal us in the drums until the welds fail. Engineered lichens could eat the perchlorates and sweeten the rain, but it takes three hundred years—and the flares kill any tender in ten.”

“Three hundred years is not never,” I said.

“To us it is,” Anya wept. “Who goes down under a poison sky to tend moss for people six generations away? Stay with Grandma Linnea tonight, Sera. I can’t tell the Council our world is dead and come home to watch her go alone.”

After Anya left, I lifted the alcove curtain.

Linnea lay under linen, seventy-four, her chest rattling with silicate lung. Sixty-four years ago, as Maren, I adopted a three-year-old orphan after the Foundry fire. For seven years she was my sun. Then I gashed my palm on a lens-blank before the surgeon; when it healed overnight, he scheduled a marrow screen. Panicking, I vented a weighted shroud through the Sluice and fled to Drum Two, leaving a ten-year-old girl screaming at the viewport where she thought her mother had become starlight.

I lasted a decade away before forging Sera’s papers and creeping back next door—watching my daughter age past me, her eyes ambering and spine bowing, never daring to touch her as more than a neighbor.

Now Linnea’s lids fluttered open. Her fingers found the locket at her throat.

“Sera?” Her breath rasped. “Don’t let them send me through the Sluice tonight.”

“Why, sweetheart?”

“Because there’s nothing left to see by,” Linnea whispered, a tear tracking her cheek. “When I was ten, they didn’t let me kiss my mama Maren before the lock cycled. For sixty-four years I’ve looked into this dark, afraid I’ve forgotten her eyes.”

A rational creature—the careful animal that survived Rome and five censuses—would have patted her hand and waited.

Instead, I bolted the shutter.

At the basin I scrubbed the alum and walnut stain from my face, pried the ambered shells from my eyes, kicked off my shimmed boots, and unpinned my white wig, letting dark hair fall to my collarbones—just as in her locket’s tintype.

When I knelt by the cot, Linnea stared through clouded lenses at a thirty-year-old woman.

To the dying, miracles are simpler than grief. Her papery hand rose to trace my unlined jaw, my brow, my clear dark eyes.

“Mama,” she breathed, softening into the child I had left in the orchard lane. “The dark didn’t take you.”

“Never,” I whispered, gathering her frail bones against my chest. “I was here the whole time, my darling.”

“You waited at the gate,” she murmured against my throat, and let her last breath go.

When Anya returned at two, the Sluice log showed two shrouds vented: Linnea’s, and Old Sera’s sixty-one kilograms of cullet and pitch.

On Anya’s table sat a Damascus fig cutting—saved from a burning garden three millennia ago—and a note in Old Sera’s hand: Spare the trees. Check the Surface Pioneer roster.

When the Samara braked above Pelagos’s violet clouds, one volunteer stood at the lander airlock: twenty-three-year-old Nora, with crates of lichen spores, the apple saplings, and lens-grinding tools.

At the viewport, Anya pressed her palm to the quartz, weeping for the stranger going down to die in the rust-storms so her distant grandchildren might smell rain. Below, I touched the fig cutting between my boots, looked out with bare eyes at the poison world that would kill me a hundred times before grass rooted, and smiled. Three hundred years was only five pairs of lenses. I would have the orchard ready for them.

Movement I81 Years on Paper

The Counterfeit Eye

Every January, Sera bakes cerium-doped hydrogel shells under ultraviolet light to match the radiation ambering of a mortal eye, preparing sixty-one kilograms of cullet and pitch for her fifth staged death.

Dilemma: Survival by vanishing every fifty years versus the ache of leaving those who stay.
Movement IIThe Long Glass

The Abiotic Mirage

Through the mirror Maren ground two lives ago, Anya resolves Pelagos’s true spectrum: ultraviolet flares splitting water into false oxygen above caustic perchlorate plains that require three centuries of lichen seeding.

Dilemma: To mortals, three hundred years is extinction; to the Council, the orchards are only phosphorus.
Movement IIIThe Alcove

Unclouded in the Lamp-Glow

Seventy-four-year-old Linnea confesses she fears the Sluice because she has forgotten the eyes of the mother who left her at ten. Sera abandons five centuries of caution, washing off her disguise to hold her dying daughter as Maren.

Dilemma: Rational self-preservation versus giving a dying child back her mother’s face.
Movement IVThe Airlock

Five Pairs of Lenses

Having boarded the Samara out of fear of being alone on a dead world, she volunteers as young Nora to descend alone onto a poison one—turning her curse into a three-hundred-year bridge for Linnea’s bloodline.

Resolution: The Damascus fig from Ugarit waits between her boots for the first sweet rain.
