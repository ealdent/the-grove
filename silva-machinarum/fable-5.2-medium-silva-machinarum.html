Perihelion · Year 312 Return of mass
Received · 60 kg

Reclamation

Everyone aboard the Perihelion borrows about sixty kilos of the ship and, at the end, gives it back. Water, carbon, a little calcium. I am the clerk who signs for the return.

I have held the post in seven rings under seven names, and six times the form I signed was my own.

It is easier than you would think. The rings rarely trade people, and nobody audits the dead. I arrive as a transfer of twenty-five with a forged slip. I grey my hair with ash from the kiln and learn, over decades, to rise from chairs slowly. Around seventy I catch a fever, file myself, weigh sixty kilos of mulch out of stock for the orchard, and walk spinward to be young somewhere else. The ship’s books balance. I am the one loan on them that has never come due.

That is the reason for the hiding, if you want one. A ship that rations water by the gram will not love a woman who never pays hers back.

I was old before the keel was laid. I remember rain. I boarded because a planet is large enough to stop pretending on, and I have wanted Morrow more than anyone aboard, which is saying something on a ship that teaches its children to draw it in blue crayon before they can write.

In my sixth life I made a mistake. I had a son.

His name is Teo. I gave him thirty-four years, and then a fever, a form, and a pear tree in the orchard with my sixth name on the tag. He cried at the tree. I watched from the ring junction with a new slip in my pocket.

I meant to go to the far side of the ship. I got as far as his corridor. The Reclamation desk in his ring was vacant, I had seen to that, and a girl called Wen took it, three doors from his observatory.

That was forty-two years ago. He is seventy-six now. I am passing for sixty-seven and doing it badly on purpose, because the older I pretend to be, the more I look like his mother. We have tea on Thirdday. He complains about calibration. Twice a year he asks me to walk to the orchard with him, and I stand beside my own tree while my son waters sixty kilos of mulch.

Teo is the one who watched Morrow go wrong. When he was forty, the water line thinned out of the spectrum, and he said it was probably the old optics. When he was sixty, the clouds resolved, and they were sulfur. Last month we came close enough to take the surface temperature directly. Four hundred degrees. Nine generations of crayon, and we have been sailing toward a kiln.

His heart gave out the week he finished the report. He sent for me because I am the clerk, and he is tidy.

“There’s another candidate,” he said. The infirmary light made him look like paper. “Two hundred and ninety years on, if we swing around Morrow’s sun and keep the speed. The numbers are fine. Nobody will take it.”

“Why not?”

“Because it isn’t a planet people need, Wen. It’s a witness. You can plant for grandchildren. You can’t plant for someone so far off that nobody who ever knew your name will be alive to say it.” He closed his eyes. “I’m going to stand up tomorrow and tell them it was for nothing, and then I’m going to die, and you’re going to file me. I’m sorry. It’s a lot of paperwork.”

Everything I have learned in a very long life said to pat his hand, sign the form, and walk spinward.

I sang instead. It was the one about the fish who wanted knees, which I made up in a kitchen forty meters from that bed, for one child, and never wrote down, and never sang again.

He opened his eyes. He looked at me the way he looks at bad data.

“I water your tree,” he said.

“I know. I’ve stood there.”

“That is a terrible thing to do to a person.”

“Yes.”

He laughed, and then it turned into the other thing, and I held my son, who was older than me in every way that showed.

After a while he was an astronomer again. “How long?”

“I remember the ocean, Teo. The real one.”

He was quiet, working it through, and I watched him arrive. “Then you’ll be there. At the next one. You’ll actually be standing there.”

“If they don’t put me out an airlock for the water I owe.”

“Mama.” Forty-two years since I had heard it. “You’re not a debt. You’re the only promise this ship has ever had that someone can keep. Come with me tomorrow. Let me give them the bad number, and then let me give them you.”

I knew what he was asking. Three centuries of being known. Every child aboard growing up aware that I would be the one to sign for them. No Morrow for me either, no wide world to vanish into. Only the corridor, and my own face, and everyone looking at it.

I said yes because he asked. I would like to claim a better reason.

He stood before the council on two canes and gave them four hundred degrees. Then he gave them his mother. It was not quiet, and it was not all kind. But the vote for the slingshot carried, and a woman I had never met took my hand afterward and said her grandmother’s name, slowly, like handing me something to hold.

Teo died nine days later. I signed his form. On the line for the clerk, I wrote the name my own mother gave me, which no one had spoken since before there were ships.

Then I carried him to the orchard and planted a pear beside the one he had watered for forty-two years.

The children come to my desk now and ask what rain was. I tell them. Then I tell them about a man who looked into a furnace and found the next star anyway, and I will still be telling it the day we arrive.

I have never paid the ship back. I have decided to carry it forward instead: every name, the whole way, to a shore.
