Vanguard · 312

The Arithmetic of Shedding Skin

Every forty-two years, I have to die in the Vanguard’s recycling vats so I can attend my own funeral from the back row.

This morning, I am seventy-one. My current name is Clara, and my hands smell of spirit gum and crushed walnut shells, the paste I rub into the creases of my knuckles to mimic the papery liver spots of the ship-born elderly. In the cramped tin mirror of Cabin 9-C, I comb titanium-white powder through roots that insist on growing in the glossy, stubborn raven they held when the Vanguard cleared the orbit of Jupiter three hundred and twelve years ago.

Mortals think immortality is a monument. It isn’t. It is housekeeping. It is remembering to limp when the damp hits the hydroponic decks because you told the apprentice botanist six years ago that your left hip was going. It is biting the inside of your cheek when that same apprentice—Julian, with the lopsided smile of his great-grandmother, Maya, whom I held as she died of radiation lung in Year 88—reaches up to take a heavy tray of dwarf-wheat seedlings from your hands because you are frail, and precious, and nearly gone.

"You shouldn't be hauling the lead flats, Clara," Julian said when I stepped into the Arboretum.

Instead of the humid green smell of misted kale, the air around his workstation smelled of ozone and cold coffee. The long holographic ribbon of the forward telescope—usually pinned above the communal tables so the children could watch New Canaan swell from a sapphire needle-prick into a marble—was dark.

"My hips have one good season left in them," I said, setting the flat down. Then I saw his face. He wasn't looking at my hands. He was staring at a peeled Mandarin orange on his console, his thumb digging into the rind until the oil beaded. "Julian?"

"The outer cloud deck isn't water vapor," he whispered. He didn't pull up the telemetry. He didn't cite the parts-per-million of hydrogen sulfide or the thermal runaway models that the probe had whispered back to the bridge at three in the morning. He just held out the bruised orange. "My grandfather spent forty years in the dark of the hull-plating crew so my mother could have an allotment of citrus trees when we landed. He never tasted one. He only kept the seeds in a locket."

He looked up at me, his eyes wet and furious, the exact hazel of Maya’s when she was nine and dropped her ration token down the floor grate.

"The planet choked while we were in the dark, Clara," he said. "A super-plume cracked the western continent two centuries after the initial survey probe left. The sky is yellow. If we open the landing bay doors, the rain will blister the paint off the hull in twenty minutes."

My knees went weak—not the practiced, theatrical wobble I had rehearsed for Clara’s upcoming fatal stroke next winter, but a hollow, vertiginous drop in my ribs.

For three centuries, I had curated them. I had been their midwife in Sector 2, their archivist in the lean years of the blight, their quiet seamstress when the looms broke. I had watched five generations of humans fold their grief into paper cranes and tuck them into the airlocks, sustained by a single, ferocious promise: our children will walk without walls.

Rationality told me what came next. The ship’s nuclear heart could burn for another four hundred years. I already had my next identity forged in the lower-deck registries—Lillian Vane, nineteen, orphan from the foundry rings. I could fake Clara’s fall down the maintenance shaft on Tuesday, scrub the walnut stain off my wrists on Wednesday, and spend the next three centuries quietly pruning dwarf wheat inside a closed tin cylinder while humanity slowly went sterile under the hum of fluorescent tubes. I didn't need a sky. My cells repaired their own telomeres; the stale recycled air of the Vanguard could never kill me.

Then Julian’s six-year-old daughter, Toma, burst through the humid curtains of the fern bay, holding a crayon drawing of a bird nobody alive had ever seen—except me. A barn swallow, copied from the archive plates, with wings twice as wide as its body.

"Look, Aunt Clara!" Toma shouted, pressing the waxy paper against my apron. "Daddy says we’re almost big enough in the window to see the beaches!"

Julian turned his face toward the bulkhead and pressed his knuckles against his mouth so she wouldn't hear him break.

Something ancient and cowardly inside me finally snapped. What was the point of hoarding an endless string of tomorrows if I spent all of them watching the people I loved press their faces against cold glass?

Wait long enough, and even stone learns to bleed.

That night, when the Emergency Directorate gathered in the observation blister beneath the yellow, bruised eye of New Canaan, I did not wear my shawl. Before I stepped through the pressure doors, I took a rag soaked in isopropyl alcohol from the med-bay and dragged it across my face. The silicone crow's feet peeled away like dead moths. The silver powder rinsed down the steel basin, leaving my hair heavy and black against my collarbones.

When I walked onto the bridge, Julian flinched backward as if a ghost had stepped out of the ship’s hull.

"Maya’s scar," he breathed, pointing a shaking finger at my left wrist—the jagged crescent from the hydroponic fire of Year 64, a scar recorded in his family’s own heirloom diary as belonging to his great-grandmother’s childhood friend, Elena.

"My name was Oona when we launched from Nairobi," I told the seven terrified faces around the table. My voice, stripped of Clara’s raspy tremor, rang out clear and terrifyingly young in the quiet dome. "And New Canaan isn't dead. It’s just indigestion."

The Chief Biologist stood up, knocking his chair back. "Who are you? How did you get in here—"

"The sulfur cascade can be scrubbed by engineered lithotrophic cyanobacteria," I said, stepping up to the glass where the toxic yellow swirls of the planet reflected in my dark eyes. "Your agricultural vaults have the base strains, but the radiation on the surface will shred their DNA before they can bloom. They need a living, self-repairing genetic template to splice into their replication cycle. Something that heals faster than cosmic rays and sulfuric acid can burn it."

I rolled up my sleeve and laid my bare, unwrinkled forearm on the glass table between the telemetry maps.

"You can't synthesize my marrow in a dish; Earth tried for forty years before the launch," I said softly, looking only at Julian. "It only replicates inside a living host. Put me in a single-seat atmospheric drop pod with the bacterial vats. Hook the dispersal injectors into my femoral artery. I’ll walk the caldera ridges and seed the lower clouds myself."

"The acid rain," Julian whispered, his voice cracking. "Even if your cells rebuild... Oona, you'll be burning and healing every second. Alone. In the yellow dark. For how long?"

"Calculations say eighty years before the sky turns blue," I lied gently. In my head, the math was closer to a hundred and forty.

Rationality is a small, dry room where nothing ever grows. Love is stepping out of the airlock without a helmet because you cannot bear the sound of a child crying in a hallway.

"Why?" the Captain asked, her hand hovering over the security alarm. "You could outlive this whole ship. Why go down into hell for us?"

I reached across the console and picked up Toma’s crumpled drawing of the barn swallow, smoothing its wax-streaked wings against my palm.

"Because I am tired of attending your funerals," I said, and for the first time in three hundred years, I smiled with my own mouth. "And when Julian’s granddaughter steps off the ramp, I want to be the one who shows her how to build a kite."
