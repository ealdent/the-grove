Later

Everyone who boarded signed a form acknowledging that they would die in transit. I signed it too. It was my first lie aboard, and it lasted three hundred and eleven years.

It helped that I was the Keeper. I washed the dead and carried them aft to the tanks, and a season later I brought them back as soil for the fields. No one looks closely at the woman who comes for the dead. Adults never visited my house unless someone had died, and children came only on dares. Every sixty years or so I grew old, then ill, then wrote my own death into the Ledger, and my granddaughter took my place. It is thought unlucky to see a Keeper’s child before she is grown. I started that. People always told her she had her grandmother’s hands.

I had died four times. I was due again in the spring, and had been coughing for the neighbors since the harvest.

From Earth you could see oxygen in the planet’s air, which usually means something is breathing. The founders were honest about the usually. They named the planet something Greek and hopeful, but the first children kept asking when they could go outside, and their parents kept saying later, and that is the name that stuck. Children painted Later on every corridor wall: blue seas, green land, a yellow sun, because that is what suns look like in books.

The real sun was red. It always had been. Later took eleven years to die, the way the old ones go, one thing at a time. First the color was wrong. Then there was no water. Then the oxygen, the whole reason we came, turned out to be more than anything alive could have made.

Maren explained it to me. The star had been brighter once, when it was young, bright enough to boil oceans, and its light had pulled the steam apart. The hydrogen drifted off into the dark. The oxygen stayed. It was never anyone’s breath. It was what a sea leaves when it dies, and it had spent a few billion years rusting everything it touched.

I knew what bodies leave. I had never thought about seas.

The children kept painting Later blue. No one had the heart to stop them. But the parents had stopped saying later. They did not know what to say instead.

Maren had come to my house on a dare when she was seven, and kept coming back for stories, as the brave ones do. I told the children about rain. I always had. They assumed I read a great deal. I had told rain to eleven generations, and carried two hundred and six of those children aft, and I kept a handful of each of them in a corner of Field Three I was not supposed to have.

Gardeners learn not to love the annuals. I never learned.

Maren came the night before the assembly, with her findings on a slate she would not look at, and asked me how you tell people that the thing they have lived for is not there.

I had told more families about death than anyone alive. “Plainly,” I said. “Once. And then you don’t leave. People can bear nearly anything if you don’t leave them alone with it.”

She nodded. Then she said, “You never would tell me what it smelled like. Rain. You said I’d know it when I smelled it.” She was crying now. “I was going to smell it on Later.”

I knew what came next. I would hold her hand. In the spring I would die, and she would bring her grief to my granddaughter, who would have to pretend to learn her name. I had three hundred and eleven years of reasons to let that happen.

“It isn’t a story,” I said. “I’ve stood in it.”

She looked at me for a long time, and then at my hands, which were shaking. She was an astronomer. She had spent her life looking at old light.

She didn’t ask how. She asked, “Can you show me?”

We walked through the quiet ship, past a hundred painted Laters, all of them blue. Field Three was dark for the night cycle, the wheat asleep. My corner had been dry for a month. I kept it that way, for the nights I needed it. I filled the watering can, lifted it high, and let the water fall through the rose. It has to fall, and gently. Poured, it is only water.

The drops found the dust, and the smell came up the way it used to come up from the hills where I was a girl, twelve light-years and a very long time ago. Maren breathed in and sat down hard in the dirt. Her body knew it before she did. I have always believed we were made to find it.

“It’s not the water you’re smelling,” I told her. “It’s what lives in the soil. Rain only smells like this where something is alive in the ground.”

She pressed her hands into the wet earth. “Then this is them.”

“This corner is the children I told about rain,” I said. “None of them ever smelled it.”

She wept, and I let her. The smell rose around us until it was all there was, and for once it was not only mine.

“Later will never smell like this,” she said.

“It will, if we bring them,” I said. “And water. And time. Not in your lifetime.”

She waited.

“Later,” I said.

It was the oldest promise on the ship. Every parent had made it to every child for three hundred years, knowing they would not be there to keep it. I would be.

She took my hand and held it until the lights came up for morning.

At the assembly she told the ship, plainly, once. When she was done, I stood up beside her without my cane and told them mine.

Then we stayed.
