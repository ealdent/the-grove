The Graft

A fruit tree will not grow true from seed. Plant the pip of a sweet Russet apple and you get crabbed, bitter wood, a stranger that remembers nothing of its mother. To keep the sweetness alive across three centuries of vacuum, you have to cut a living slip from the old tree, slice a notch into a younger rootstock, and bind the two wounds together with beeswax twine until their veins knit and agree to be one life.

That was how I survived nine of them.

My first name on The Almanac was Hannah, back when the hull still smelled of Earth’s dry-dock grease and the air tasted faintly of cedar instead of recycled rain. After the coronal storm in Year 64 chewed the ship’s digital census to static, the four habitat rings went back to rag-paper ledgers kept in ink that faded to rust. After that, disappearing was mostly posture and patience.

Every thirty-five years, I worked wood-ash into my temples and wore a weighted linen brace beneath my tunic until my shoulders curved with the polite stoop of age. When my time in a ring grew thin, I slipped a spare brass death-chit into the Reclamation soil manifest—no one audits the compost vats too closely—washed the ash from my hair in a dark washroom, straightened my spine, and walked through the bulkhead into another ring with a fresh name.

You have your grandmother’s hands, people would tell me, smiling over bowls of turnip broth. You laugh just like your mother did.

In a closed ship of three thousand souls, faces echo. Nobody looks twice at a girl who wears the dead.

The rule was simple: never stay long enough for anyone to notice the clock had stopped on my skin. Love lightly. Prune early. Move on before the roots tangled.

I kept the rule for two hundred and sixty years. Then I met a hull-welder in Ring Two with ozone in his collar and a gap between his front teeth, and I forgot how to be sensible.

The morning Sola died, nobody screamed.

I had expected riots, or the brittle hysteria that broke out in Century Two when the water-scrubbers failed in Ring Three. Instead, when the Captain’s voice cracked through the brass corridors—steady at first, then hollow as a struck pipe—the ship simply stopped moving.

For three hundred and eighteen years, Sola had been a pale turquoise bead in the forward glass, verified by Earth’s last great mirrors: liquid sheen, oxygen signatures, a cradle waiting fourteen months past our deceleration burn. What the old mirrors hadn’t known—what only our own probes saw once we cleared the outer dust—was that Sola’s iron heart had cooled long before our ancestors ever lit the fusion candles. Without a magnetic shield, the solar wind had skinned the sky raw. The oxygen was locked in dead rust. The blue wasn’t water; it was frozen ammonia-brine crusted over salt flats under an unblinking scour of radiation.

Nothing with lungs or roots could live there.

The Captain laid the arithmetic before us like a cold knife. We had reaction mass for one burn. We could brake into orbit around a corpse and live out the hull’s last seventy years in quiet decline, letting the human race go to sleep in the dark. Or we could use Sola’s dead gravity as a sling and hurl ourselves toward Vesper, a dim orange star four hundred years further out, unmapped and unpromised, on a ship whose bones already groaned when the thrusters coughed.

In the Orchard, beneath the amber sun-lamps, a girl named Lina lowered her pruning shears to the dirt. She was nineteen, with the exact crooked eyebrow of a boy I had danced with during the Year 18 solstice.

“What’s the point?” she said, not to me, just to the damp air. “Ten generations in a tin pipe. My grandfather lost three fingers welding the outer seam so I could walk on grass.” She rubbed her knuckles against her eyes, leaving streaks of loam. “Even if we turn for Vesper, we’ll all be dust before we get halfway. Nobody will even remember who kept the lamps lit.”

She left the shears in the soil and walked away. All down the terrace, people were setting down watering cans, folding themselves onto the benches, staring up through the curved glass at the tiny turquoise lie.

I picked up a small, bruised Russet from the basket at my feet and went to find Tomas.

He was eighty-two now, bundled in a quilt patched from old engineering coveralls, parked in his wheeled chair beneath the oldest grafted tree in Ring Two. His breathing sounded like dry leaves dragged across metal.

Eight years ago, when he was seventy-four and his wife of thirty years had finally gone to the Loam, I had broken every sensible law I lived by and moved back into Ring Two. I had told him I was Nora, the orphaned grand-niece of a woman he had loved in his youth. Eight years is safe. Eight years turns a face from twenty-eight to thirty-six, if you keep your eyes tired and don’t smile too brightly.

I had told myself I only came back to carry his tea and listen to his stories. That was a lie. I came back because I had spent thirty-eight years in Ring Four trying to forget the shape of his hands, and I had failed.

“Did you hear?” Tomas asked without opening his eyes.

“I heard.” I sat on the upturned crate beside his chair.

“My great-granddaughter is four,” he murmured. His fingers plucked weakly at a loose thread on his quilt. “I wanted her to hear rain without a ceiling over it. I wanted her to know the difference between a floor and the ground.”

He opened his eyes. They were milky at the rims, the dark brown gone pale as steeped tea. “Three hundred years, Nora. We carried a song all this way just to sing it to an empty room. And when I’m gone next month, or next week, there won’t even be anyone left who remembers Clara’s voice. Just iron, cooling down.”

Something inside my ribs gave way—an old wax seal cracking after three centuries of pressure.

The rational thing was obvious. Every survival instinct honed across nine funerals screamed it at me: Hold his hand. Be Nora. Let him drift off gently. If the ship votes to slingshot into the dark, you will need a clean ledger and no ghosts whispering your name. A secret kept for three hundred years does not survive a whisper in an infirmary ward. If people learned what I was, they would either hate me for outliving their children or cut me open looking for a miracle I didn’t know how to give.

I looked at Tomas’s papery hands—the faint white scar across his left knuckle where a plasma spark had kissed him in Year 263, when I was thirty-one on paper and he was twenty-seven and fearless. I thought of the morning in Year 272 when I had seen the first silver hairs beside his ears, and heard him murmur in the dark, You never seem to tire, do you?—and how my cowardice had sent me down to the grey-water sluice six months later to stage my own drowning. I had stood behind a rusted pillar and watched him weep at the Memorial Wall until his knees gave out.

I had let him carry that hole in his chest for forty-six years so I could stay safe.

Safe for what? To sit alone in an empty ship and survive the silence?

I pulled my pocketknife from my apron and lifted the bruised Russet. Slowly, without looking away from his face, I began to peel it—turning the apple against the blade in one unbroken curl of red skin, flicking my wrist upward at the stem so the ribbon wouldn’t snap.

Tomas’s hand stopped moving on the quilt.

He stared at the coiled peel falling into my lap. Then his milky eyes climbed slowly to my face, searching the line of my jaw, the faint crescent scar beneath my thumb where a blackberry thorn had caught me before The Almanac ever left Earth’s sky.

“The night the Ring Two heaters failed in sixty-five,” I said softly, my voice shaking for the first time in a century. “I couldn’t feel my toes. You pulled my feet inside your shirt, right against your ribs, and you swore that when we reached Sola you’d build me a porch out of real timber. Not a bulkhead rail. Something that gave splinters.”

Silence pooled beneath the apple tree. Somewhere overhead, a circulation fan ticked once, twice, steady as a pulse.

Tomas didn’t ask how. He didn’t shrink back in awe, or demand equations for the trick in my blood. His mouth trembled, and a sound tore out of him that was half a laugh and half a sob—the sound of a man setting down a weight he had carried for half a century.

“You didn’t drown,” he whispered.

“No,” I said, tears finally spilling hot down my cheeks. “I was a coward, Tomas. I didn’t know how to stay and watch you turn gray.”

He reached out, slow and unsteady, and laid his cool, spotted palm against my face. “Oh, Clara,” he breathed. “My stubborn, foolish girl. How lonely have you been?”

I leaned my cheek into his hand and wept for the first time since Earth still had tides.

For a long while, neither of us spoke. The amber lamps above the Orchard shifted into their evening dim, casting long, gentle shadows between the dwarf trees. Up through the viewport, Sola hung like a cold drop of spilled paint.

“Four hundred years to Vesper,” Tomas murmured at last, his thumb resting against my cheekbone. His gaze drifted up to the dark glass, and then back to my face—and I watched the understanding bloom behind his cloudy eyes, bright and fierce and strange. “Nobody alive will see it. Nobody will know who patched the seams, or planted the peaches, or loved who in the dark.”

I caught his frail hands in mine and pressed them flat against my chest, over a heart that had been beating since oceans still had whales.

“I will,” I told him.

His breath hitched.

“I can’t make Sola bloom,” I whispered. “I can’t stop tomorrow from taking you. That’s the cruelest part of what I am—I can’t save a single one of you. All I can do is stay.” I tightened my fingers around his. “I remember your mother’s off-key hymn, Tomas. I remember the name of the first child born in Ring One, and the smell of hot asphalt after July rain, and the exact way you used to hum when you thought nobody was listening. If this ship turns into the dark, I will walk every mile of it. I will carry your name to the shore, even if it’s four hundred years away. Even if it takes a thousand.”

A quiet, luminous peace settled over the deep grooves of his face. “Tell them,” he said softly, his eyelids drifting shut, his fingers curling inside mine. “Not what you are—keep yourself safe, love. Just tell them the porch has to face the morning light.”

“I promise,” I whispered.

I sat with him until his breathing slowed into sleep, his hand warm inside my own. Above us, the ship’s intercom chimed softly—the Captain calling the rings to the evening assembly, where three thousand frightened mortals would decide whether to die beside a ruin or wager everything on another four hundred years of dark.

When I finally stood, tucking the quilt tight around Tomas’s shoulders, Lina was walking slowly back down the terrace to collect her dropped shears. She paused beside the old Russet tree, looking at my wet face, then at the small knife still open in my hand.

“Why are you still working?” she asked quietly.

I reached up, cut a tender green shoot from the crown of the ancient tree, and notched a young sapling beside it. My hands were steady. I picked up a length of beeswax twine and held one end out to her.

“Because the tree doesn’t know how far the shore is,” I said. “And because I remember how rain smells on warm dirt, Lina. Hold this tight for me—we’re going to need apples when we get there.”

She stared at me for a heartbeat, her crooked eyebrow drawing together at the strangeness of my words. Then, slowly, she stepped forward and pressed her thumb down over the knot, binding the old life into the new.

    Tomas is born in Ring Two.

  4. Years 260–272

    Ring Two, as Clara. A plasma spark on his left knuckle; silver at his temples; a staged drowning in the grey-water sluice.

  5. Years 272–309

    Thirty-seven years in Ring Four, as Sera.

  6. Year 310

    Return to Ring Two as Nora, Clara’s grand-niece. Tomas is seventy-four.

  7. Year 318

    Fourteen months from deceleration. Sola’s magnetic core is cold; the blue is frozen ammonia-brine. Ahead in the dark: Vesper, four hundred years out.
