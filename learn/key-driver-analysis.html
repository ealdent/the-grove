    Analyst's field guide · no regression required

    Key Driver
    Analysis

    Key driver analysis finds which inputs matter most for an outcome you care about — so you can spend your one budget on the one thing that moves it.

    You run a small coffee-shop chain. Last month you surveyed 240 customers. Each one rated overall satisfaction from 0 to 10, and then rated five things about the visit — also 0 to 10:

    • Taste of the coffee
    • Price fairness
    • Speed of service
    • Staff friendliness
    • Cleanliness

    Your budget covers improving exactly one of them this year. Better beans, or a second espresso machine, or a price cut, or barista training, or a cleaning contract. Pick wrong and you have spent the year politely.

    Which lever moves overall satisfaction the most? That question is key driver analysis. Everything below is how you answer it without fooling yourself.

    02 · the anchor

    A two-minute correlation refresher

    Correlation, written r, is one number summarising how two measurements move together. It runs from −1 to +1. Drag the slider to set the true relationship and watch the cloud respond.

    Direction

    Positive r: they rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls. The sign is the arrow.

    Strength

    Near ±1 the cloud is a tight line. Near 0 it is a blob. Strength is tightness, not steepness.

    Correlation only sees straight lines. Press the button above. That U-shaped cloud has an obvious, strong pattern and a correlation of about zero. r answers “how well does a straight line describe this?” — not “is there a relationship?”

    park the slider at 0.30 and then 0.70. Survey drivers usually land in that range — strong enough to be real, loose enough that ranking them by eye is hopeless.

    03 · the trap

    The obvious answer, and why it is wrong

    Here is the first thing anyone does: correlate each of the five drivers with overall satisfaction, then rank them. Five correlations, computed from the table in section 1.

    Correlation of each driver with overall satisfaction

    That certainly looks like an answer. Price fairness leads, cleanliness trails, ship it. Except every one of those five numbers was computed as if the other four did not exist — and in survey data, they very much do.

    The drivers are correlated with each other

    Hover any cell. This is the same five drivers correlated against themselves.

    Intercorrelations · driver × driver

    Two things are happening in that grid, and both are normal.

    The halo effect. A customer who had a great visit rates everything a bit high, and a customer whose order was wrong rates everything a bit low. Their general mood leaks into every answer. That shared mood puts a floor of roughly 0.3 under every pair in the grid — it is a property of the respondent, not of your coffee.

    Genuine overlap. Staff friendliness and cleanliness sit at 0.71. A shop that feels well-run feels well-run on both counts, and customers can barely separate them.

    Why this breaks the ranking. Suppose cleanliness has no independent effect at all — customers only notice it as part of “this place is well-run”, which they also express as staff friendliness. Cleanliness will still correlate handsomely with overall satisfaction, because it is riding along with something that does matter. Ranking by raw correlations hands out the same credit five separate times. Add up those five numbers and you get more explanation than the outcome contains.
    04 · the fix, part one

    Multiple regression, held at arm's length

    Multiple regression asks all five drivers the question at once, so each one has to earn credit the others cannot already supply. The output we want is the standardized beta, written β. Read it like this:

    β for Price fairness = 0.37 means: nudge a customer's price rating up by one standard deviation — while their four other ratings stay exactly where they are — and their overall satisfaction goes up about 0.37 standard deviations.

    The phrase carrying all the weight is while the others stay fixed. That is what a plain correlation cannot do, and it is the entire reason regression is here. Standardized means every driver is measured in its own standard deviations, so the five betas are comparable to each other even though price ratings are more spread out than taste ratings.

    Raw correlation vs. standardized beta
    correlation with overall (each driver alone) standardized β (all five together)

    Every dot moves left, which is the double-counting draining out. But look at cleanliness: a perfectly respectable correlation of 0.52 collapses to a beta of 0.03.

    Cleanliness is not unimportant. It is redundant. Once the model already knows how a customer rated staff friendliness — its partner at r = 0.71 — the cleanliness rating has almost nothing left to add. Regression is ruthless about this: when two drivers carry the same information, it gives the credit to whichever one predicts a sliver better and leaves the other holding nothing. In this dataset cleanliness genuinely has no independent effect — but a beta near zero never tells you which of those two stories you are in.

    One more number: R²

    R² is how much of the variation in overall satisfaction all five drivers explain together. Here R² = 0.76, so about 76% of why customers differ in satisfaction is captured by these five ratings; the rest is things you did not ask about, plus noise. Hold onto R² — the next section carves it into five pieces.

    So why not just ship the betas? Because a beta of 0.03 reads as “cleanliness does not matter”, and if you act on that by cancelling the cleaner you will find out otherwise. Overlapping drivers make betas a winner-take-all report on shared credit. We need a rule for splitting shared credit instead.
    05 · the centerpiece

    Fair credit: the Shapley split

    Three people move a sofa. Ana can shift it alone, Ben can shift it alone, Cai only helps when someone else is already lifting. How much did each contribute? Asking “what happens if we remove Ana?” undersells her, because Ben quietly covers for her. Asking “what can Ana do alone?” oversells her, because she and Ben would be double-counted.

    Lloyd Shapley's answer, from 1953: line the team up in every possible order, and pay each person the average of what they added when they walked in. Sometimes you arrive first and get full credit for the whole lift. Sometimes you arrive last, find the sofa nearly up, and add little. Average over all the orders and the overlap gets split instead of awarded.

    Swap “how much of the sofa moved” for R² and you have key driver analysis. Let us do it by hand with three drivers.

    Step 1 · R² of every possible team · Taste, Price, Speed

    Eight teams, including the empty one. Each box is a separate small regression: the R² you get from exactly those drivers and nothing else.

    Notice T alone reaches 0.42 and P alone reaches 0.47, but together they only reach 0.62 — not 0.89. The missing 0.27 is the overlap they share. Shapley's job is to divide it.

    Step 2 · walk one join-order

    Pick an order, then step through it. Each driver's contribution is whatever R² it added at the moment it walked in.

    walk T → P → S, then walk S → P → T. Taste is worth 0.42 in the first order and 0.10 in the last. Neither number is “the” importance of taste — that is exactly the problem Shapley solves.

    Step 3 · average over all six orders

    Walk an order above to fill in its row, or fill in all six at once.

    The three column averages are the Shapley importances. And they add up to exactly the R² of the full three-driver model — no credit invented, none lost. That property is why this method won: the pie is the right size, and every slice is defensible.

    All five drivers

    With five drivers there are 32 teams and 120 orders. Your laptop does not care; the page computed it before you finished this sentence.

    Shapley importance · five drivers · shares of R²

    Read cleanliness again. Regression gave it β = 0.03 — effectively nothing. Shapley gives it 10% of the explained variation. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Regression asks “what does cleanliness add last, after everything else?” Shapley asks “what does cleanliness add on average, whenever it arrives?” For prioritising a budget across overlapping levers, the second question is the one you meant.
    Names in the wild. The same computation is sold and published as LMG (Lindeman–Merenda–Gold), dominance analysis, Shapley value regression, and — loosely, via a slightly different algorithm that usually lands close — Johnson's relative weights. If a market-research vendor hands you “relative importance, sums to 100%”, this is almost always what is underneath.
    06 · the deliverable

    Importance × performance

    Importance alone does not tell you where to spend. A driver can be the most important thing in the model and already be excellent — improving it is expensive and nobody notices. You need importance and current performance, on one chart.

    Performance is the plain average rating. Importance is the Shapley share. Cross them and the action is in the corners.

    Fix first — matters, and you are bad at it Protect — matters, and you are good at it Low priority — weak lever, weak scores Don't over-invest — already fine, small lever

    What-if: drag a driver's performance

    The dividing lines are the average performance across the five drivers (vertical) and an equal 20% share of importance (horizontal) — so the quadrants are relative to this shop, not absolute standards. The sliders move performance only; importance stays fixed, because it comes from the survey you already ran.

    In this dataset the verdict is unambiguous: price fairness is the single most important driver and the worst-rated. It is the only thing in the fix-first corner. Taste and staff friendliness are load-bearing and already strong — protect them, do not congratulate yourself into a redesign. Cleanliness scores well and moves little; keep it clean and stop there.

    Careful with the fix-first corner. A low average can mean you are genuinely bad at it, or that the attribute is intrinsically hard to score well on — almost nobody rates price fairness a 10 anywhere. Compare against competitor benchmarks or your own history before reading a low mean as a failure.

    crank the halo to 0.8 and re-sample. Every correlation inflates, R² looks fantastic, and the recovered shares flatten toward equal — the halo makes the drivers so alike that no method can tell them apart. Good survey design beats clever statistics.

    set one driver's true weight to 0 while leaving the halo at 0.45. It still shows up with a real correlation and a non-trivial Shapley share. That is not a bug in Shapley; it is what shared-mood variance looks like from the inside.

    08 · the fine print

    Six ways this goes wrong

    Correlation is not causation

    Every number on this page came from watching, not intervening. “Customers who rate price fairly are more satisfied” is not “cutting prices raises satisfaction”. Satisfied customers may simply feel generous about price.

    Do: use KDA to pick what to test. Then A/B test it in a few stores.
    Halo and common-method bias

    One person answered every question in one mood, in one sitting, on one scale. Some of what looks like “drivers of satisfaction” is just that mood echoing through the questionnaire.

    Do: pair survey ratings with hard data — measured wait times, actual prices, audit scores.
    Multicollinearity

    When two drivers overlap heavily, regression hands the shared credit to whichever wins by a hair. Cleanliness got β = 0.03 here. Re-sample the survey and staff friendliness could be the one holding nothing.

    Do: check the intercorrelation grid first. If a pair is above 0.7, report them as one theme, and never rank on betas alone.
    Range restriction

    If everyone rates taste 9 or 10, taste has almost no variance — and a driver that does not vary cannot explain variation. It will look unimportant precisely because you are excellent at it.

    Do: print the standard deviation next to every mean. A driver with sd under about 0.8 is being suppressed by its own ceiling.
    Small samples

    Importance estimates are noisy, and Shapley shares are ratios of noisy things. Section 7 lets you watch a 30-person study invent a different winner every draw.

    Do: aim for 30–50 respondents per driver. Bootstrap the importances and show the intervals, not just the bars.
    Importance is relative to the model

    These five drivers are the only candidates in the room. If the real reason people love the place is the patio, and you never asked about the patio, its credit gets smeared across whatever correlates with it.

    Do: run open-ended questions or interviews before fixing the attribute list. And re-check the list every wave.

    10 · take this with you

    The whole workflow on one card

    Key driver analysis · procedure

    1. Name one outcome. Overall satisfaction, NPS, renewal intent — one column, and it must vary.
    2. List candidate drivers from qualitative work, not from the dashboard you happen to own. Missing drivers do not stay missing; their credit gets handed to their neighbours.
    3. Look at the intercorrelation grid before anything else. Anything above 0.7 is one theme wearing two hats. Decide now whether to merge it.
    4. Fit the standardized regression for the “holding the others fixed” view, and note R². Do not rank on betas when drivers overlap.
    5. Compute Shapley / LMG importance for the ranking you will actually present. It splits shared credit and sums to R², so shares of 100% are honest.
    6. Plot importance against performance. The fix-first corner is your recommendation. One chart, four labels, no table.
    7. Sanity-check the sample. Roughly 30–50 respondents per driver; bootstrap intervals if you can. Quote ranks, not decimals.
    8. Then go prove it. Change the fix-first driver in a few locations, hold the rest, and measure. KDA points the experiment; the experiment is what earns the budget.
