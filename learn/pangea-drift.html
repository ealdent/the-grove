    How to read this

    What you are looking at

    A globe, drawn entirely in characters. It is an orthographic projection: you are looking straight down the axis of the Earth from space, and the horizon is the bright ring around the disc. Drag to spin it; the far side of the planet is genuinely out of sight, not faded out.

    Every glyph is one patch of the Earth's surface. Cells are laid out on concentric rings of constant solid angle rather than on a flat lat/lon grid, which is why they stay square on the ground and squeeze towards the limb instead of shearing. Each is coloured by which continent's crust sits on it and stamped with a character whose density tracks how far inland you are, so coastlines read as edges, continental interiors as solid mass, and the seafloor as depth bands measured from distance to the nearest coast. The highlight towards the top-left is a fixed specular sheen, added so the disc reads as a lit sphere; it is a lighting effect, not a solar terminator.

    Because the projection is orthographic, area near the limb is compressed, so the disc is foreshortened rather than equal-area. Read the geography, not the areas.

    Where the data comes from

    The rotation model is built on Müller et al. (2016), Domeier & Torsvik (2014) and Matthews et al. (2016). Coastline geometry is the model's own present-day coastline layer, simplified to 0.18° of a degree.

    What is exact and what is not

    • Exact: at 0 Ma the reconstruction is the modern world, identically. Plates stay rigid, and the North Atlantic opens to the correct geometry — Boston sits against Africa's west margin by 200 Ma, with the Pangaea-closure pole at 64.3°N, 14.7°W.
    • Africa is the reference. Every rotation is composed relative to plate 701 and Africa is held at its present position, which is the frame published paleogeographic maps use.
    • True polar wander is excluded on purpose. The source model also ships hotspot-frame and longitude-shift corrections; those are a frame correction, not continental drift, so they are not applied.
    • Deep time is approximate. The published animation reconstructs topological plate polygons with half-stage spreading. This atlas reduces the same rotations to rigid coastlines, which cannot reproduce every plate at 250–330 Ma. The time range stops at 250 Ma, the best-constrained window and the one this tutorial is about. Treat pre-Cretaceous positions of plates outside the North Atlantic as indicative, not authoritative.

    Space play / pause

    +− zoom in / out

    G guided tour
    [] slower / faster
    , jump to earlier time

    ? this panel

    Mouse: drag to spin the globe, wheel to zoom (the point under the cursor stays put). Touch: one finger spins, two fingers pinch to zoom. Both work anywhere on the disc. Z cycles the character size, which is the resolution control — smaller characters mean more of the planet per character cell.
