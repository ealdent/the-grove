WHEN THE INSTRUCTIONS CHANGE

The problem is in
the making.

DNA tells cells how to grow, mature and stop. Changes picked up during life can disrupt those instructions. [1] [2]

COMPARE THE MARROW

Select a specimen to compare.
Watch cells develop, then compare what happens. Cell numbers and timing are illustrative.

Why did this happen?

Usually, there is no single identifiable cause. DNA changes can accumulate as blood-forming cells age. Prior cancer treatment, smoking and certain chemical exposures can increase risk, but a risk factor is not an explanation for an individual case.

Most changes are acquired during life, rather than inherited. Rare inherited predispositions exist. These diseases are not contagious, and the diagnosis does not mean someone did something wrong.

What are “blasts,” and what does 20% mean?

Blasts are very immature blood cells. A small number is normal in marrow. In AML, abnormal blasts accumulate and cannot do the work of mature cells.

For many cases, at least 20% blasts in blood or marrow supports an AML diagnosis. Certain genetic findings can establish AML below that level. Classification systems also differ near the boundary. A specialist interprets the whole pattern; a percentage alone cannot diagnose it.

MDS risk tools combine cell counts, marrow findings, chromosome changes and, in some tools, gene mutations. “Higher risk” describes a greater chance of serious problems or progression—not a certainty.

04

MAKING SENSE OF THE NAMES

Two clues
in each name.

Leukemias are cancers of blood-forming cells. The names describe the cell family and how the disease tends to behave. [4]

MYELOID FAMILYRed cells, platelets & some white cellsLYMPHOID FAMILYOther white cellsAcuteUsually develops quicklyChronicOften develops more slowly

MDS belongs to the myeloid group but is a distinct diagnosis. It is not another name for AML. “Acute” and “chronic” are not stages.

05

DIFFERENT WAYS TO HELP

Change the course.
Support the person.

Treatment depends on the disease’s biology, overall health and what matters to the person—not age alone. [5]

An alternative to intensive chemotherapy is not always chemotherapy-free. Explore what each approach actually does.

Can treatment be chemotherapy-free?

Sometimes, for a specific disease and situation. Some mutation-matched drugs can be used alone. APL, a special subtype of AML, is often treated with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide; some cases also need chemotherapy. APL needs urgent specialist care because dangerous bleeding can occur.

For most other AML, there is no universal chemotherapy-free substitute. “Targeted,” “oral” and “outpatient” do not mean mild or risk-free. Ask which drugs in a proposed plan are chemotherapy and what benefit each adds. [1] [6]

What about clinical trials and immune treatments?

A clinical trial may offer a new drug or combination at diagnosis or later. Availability depends on the disease and the study’s eligibility rules.

Some AML treatments use antibodies to deliver a cell-killing drug. CAR T-cell treatment, familiar from some other blood cancers, is not a routine established treatment for AML or MDS; immune approaches are being studied. Ask a leukemia specialist about current trials. [12]

KNOW WHEN TO CALL

Fever can be urgent.

During treatment, call the care team immediately for 38°C / 100.4°F or higher, or the threshold they give you. Call for new signs of infection even without fever. Do not wait until the next appointment. [9]

Severe trouble breathing, new confusion or bleeding that will not stop: seek emergency care. [10]
