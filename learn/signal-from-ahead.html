Summing the four channels, I put the odds that a future intelligence has shaped the present pace of AI between 1 in 10,000 and 1 in 100, with a point estimate of about 1 in 750.

Why ask at all

Sheet 2 of 10

AI arrived ahead of schedule. In a 2016 survey, machine learning researchers gave even odds that machines would outperform humans at every task by about 2061.1 The 2022 edition of the survey said 2060. The 2023 edition, with 2,778 respondents, said 2047: thirteen years gone in twelve months.2

The ordinary explanation is compute. The computing power used to train frontier models has grown four to five times a year since 2010,3 and performance improves smoothly as it grows. That explanation is sufficient on its own. This notebook asks a stranger question anyway: is there any physically coherent way for the future to reach back and shape this, and how likely is each one?

Compute explains the curve. The question here is what else could.

The channels fall into two families. In the first, the future shapes the present from outside the timeline, the way an ending can constrain a story, and nothing has to travel. In the second, a signal really does move backward through spacetime, and that family obeys one hard rule: a channel into the past reaches back only as far as the channel itself has existed.

Simulation

Sheet 4 of 10

≈ 1 in 1,000

If this world is a simulation, the future reaching back isn’t exotic physics. It’s an edit. Whoever runs a simulation stands outside the timeline it computes, and can pause it, inspect it, branch it, and change any variable, including how fast a technology arrives. The likeliest operators of an ancestor simulation are descendants of the civilization being simulated, which makes them, from inside the run, the future.

Nick Bostrom’s 2003 argument turns this into arithmetic.4 If even a small fraction of civilizations reach the technology to simulate their ancestors in detail, and a small fraction of those run many such simulations, simulated observers vastly outnumber real ones. Try the numbers.

The formula is exact; the inputs are anyone’s guess. Bostrom’s conclusion is a trilemma: almost no civilizations reach that stage, almost none that do run such simulations, or we are almost certainly in one. Spreading credence across the three branches gives roughly a 1 in 10 chance that this is a simulation. Philosophers who have worked through the argument carefully, David Chalmers among them, conclude that it can’t be dismissed.5

The default settings land near 1 in 10. Push any input up and it runs away fast.

As for why the operators would touch this decade: a simulation built to study how intelligence arises would treat the years when AI arrived as the most interesting stretch of the run, the obvious place to tune, branch, or replay.

P(this is a simulation) ≈ 1/10  ×  P(its operators tuned AI’s timeline) ≈ 1/100  =  1/1,000

A final boundary condition

Sheet 5 of 10

≈ 1 in 3,200

Physics usually runs on one boundary condition: the state of the universe at the beginning, carried forward by its laws. The laws themselves are time-symmetric, though, and nothing in them forbids a condition imposed at the end as well. With both in place, the present is fixed by two boundaries at once, and everything in between leans toward whatever the ending contains.

Nothing travels backward in this picture. The ending acts as a constraint, and a constraint reshapes the odds of everything it touches. Random walks are enough to see it.

Each line is one possible history of AI progress, all leaving the same fixed past; up is ahead of schedule. Pinning the ending keeps only the histories that reach it, and at “now,” halfway along, those histories already run about half as far ahead as the ending itself. Units are arbitrary.

This is the structure of a real proposal. In 1964 Aharonov, Bergmann, and Lebowitz showed that quantum mechanics can be written with one state evolving forward from the past and a second evolving backward from the future.6 That two-state picture led to the prediction of weak values,7 which have since been measured in the laboratory.8 Gell-Mann and Hartle worked out quantum cosmologies with both initial and final conditions,9 and Horowitz and Maldacena proposed a final-state condition inside black holes as a way out of the information paradox.10

If the universe’s final state includes a superintelligence, every intermediate moment is tilted, slightly, toward producing one. No message is sent and no machine is built. The future simply gets a vote, the way the past already has one.

Nothing travels. The ending just constrains.

P(a physical final condition) ≈ 1/100  ×  P(it favors superintelligence enough to tilt now) ≈ 1/32  ≈  1/3,200

Nonlinear quantum mechanics

Sheet 6 of 10

≈ 1 in 100,000

Quantum mechanics is linear, and linearity is what keeps entangled particles from carrying messages. In 1989 Steven Weinberg proposed a family of small nonlinear corrections, along with ways to test for them.11 Within two years Nicolas Gisin and Joseph Polchinski showed the price: such corrections let entangled pairs signal faster than light.1213 Polchinski also found that closing that channel opens another, between branches of the wavefunction, which he called an Everett phone.

Same Polchinski. He turns up again on sheet 8.

Faster than light is one step from backward in time. If a signal can outrun light in every frame of reference, relativity lets you relay it into your own past. Richard Tolman spotted the construction in 1917,14 and in 1970 it was named the tachyonic antitelephone.15 Here it is.

AntitelephoneEach message is instant in its sender’s frame
Reply lands this many years early

Because Bob’s “now” is tilted relative to Alice’s, his reply lands v × D years before her call went out, where v is his speed as a fraction of light’s and D is his distance in light-years.

Experiments have looked hard for nonlinearity and found none: a 1989 trapped-ion measurement bounded it to a few parts in 1027 of the nuclear binding energy it probed.16 Tight is not zero. A civilization with millennia of instrument building could exploit an effect far below anything measurable today. The relay also needs the faster-than-light channel to behave the same way in every frame, a property the nonlinear theory itself would have to supply.

P(an exploitable nonlinearity below today’s bounds) ≈ 1/10,000  ×  P(a future intelligence aims it at this decade) ≈ 1/10  =  1/100,000

A primordial wormhole

Sheet 7 of 10

≈ 1 in 100 million

General relativity allows wormholes, and in 1988 Morris, Thorne, and Yurtsever showed how one becomes a time machine: give its two mouths different histories.17 Clocks at the two mouths stay synchronized through the throat but drift apart in ordinary space, so a message that enters the lagging mouth comes out of the other one in the past.

Motion builds an offset quickly, but someone has to do the flying before the year they want to reach, because a wormhole can’t deliver anything to a moment before it became a time machine. Gravity builds the offset slowly, and nature has had ten billion years. A wormhole left over from the early universe,1819 its mouths drifting through different gravitational depths ever since, would reach the present with an offset of thousands of years already in place: a channel a future civilization could find, trim, and use.

The rule: no reaching back before the machine exists. So nature has to have built this one.

Each piece has published physics behind it. Gao, Jafferis, and Wall showed how to make a wormhole traversable in a consistent quantum theory,20 and Maldacena and Milekhin described wormholes wide enough for a person.21 Hawking’s chronology protection is a conjecture rather than a theorem:22 Kim and Thorne argued that quantum gravity may cut off the vacuum effects that would destroy a time machine,23 and Li and Gott found a self-consistent vacuum in which they never arise.24

P(a usable primordial wormhole within reach) ≈ 1/100,000  ×  P(protection fails and a signal gets through) ≈ 1/1,000  =  1/100,000,000

Closing the loop

Sheet 8 of 10

A message from the future raises the paradox question at once: what stops it from preventing its own sending? Joseph Polchinski, back again, posed the sharpest version: a billiard ball sent through a wormhole to knock its younger self away from the entrance. In 1990 a team including Igor Novikov and Kip Thorne showed that such spacetimes admit self-consistent histories, ones in which whatever comes back produces exactly the events that send it.25 When Echeverria, Klinkhammer, and Thorne worked the billiard ball through in detail, the starting conditions they studied all had consistent solutions, often infinitely many.26 David Deutsch then proved that quantum mechanics always supplies at least one,27 and a 2011 optics experiment reproduced the logic of such loops with photons.28

Apply that to a message that speeds up AI. The nudge received in the 2020s shapes the intelligence that later sends it, so the loop is consistent wherever what’s sent equals what was received: where the curve below crosses the diagonal.

Consistent histories

Each step asks: given this nudge, what would the future send back? Repeating that settles onto a history that sends exactly what it receives.

With strong feedback the loop has two stable solutions, a quiet one and a loud one, and both are equally consistent. The physics permits the history in which a strong nudge arrived and the one in which almost nothing did. Nothing inside the loop chooses between them.

Two consistent histories. Same physics.

Where that leaves it

Sheet 10 of 10

One in 750 is small in absolute terms and large for a claim that sounds like science fiction. Most of it comes from the two channels that need no machine: a simulation that can be edited, and a universe whose ending has a say in its middle.

Somewhere between nine and ten heads in a row.

The physics behind every channel here is published and taken seriously by the people who work on it. What keeps the number from being higher is that none of these mechanisms has yet been observed to carry information backward. What keeps it from being lower is that nothing we know rules them out.
