THE STATE VECTOR

First, know
where you are.

Before you can calculate a destination, you need a starting condition.

A trajectory is a prediction of position through time. Give Newton’s equations a spacecraft’s position, its velocity, and the time of that measurement in a chosen reference frame, and a gravity model lets you predict where it will go next.

NASA’s ground network measured radio range and Doppler shift. Flight controllers fitted those observations to a trajectory to improve the spacecraft’s state estimate. On board, inertial instruments and optical observations supported navigation and guidance. [1]

Onboard computers guided the burns: Saturn’s launch vehicle computer controlled translunar injection; the spacecraft’s guidance computers handled later maneuvers. Ground computers and people planned and refined the targets. The trip depended on both.

03

THE MOVING TARGET

Aim where the Moon
is going to be.

You cannot fly to yesterday’s coordinates.

During a three-day coast, the Moon moves roughly 40 degrees around Earth. Launch time, departure direction, energy, and travel time must agree on the same encounter.

Engineers treated this as a targeting problem: choose a departure state, predict the arrival, measure the miss, and adjust the departure. They adjusted departure time, direction, and velocity until the predicted lunar encounter met the mission’s constraints. [3]

The lab’s minimum-energy path takes nearly five days to reach lunar distance. Apollo’s roughly three-day outbound trip used a faster transfer. “Just reach the Moon’s orbit” and “meet the Moon on schedule” are different questions.

THE CALCULATION LOOP

Start with an ellipse.
End with a prediction.

The elegant picture is a beginning. The operational answer needs more.

A useful first approximation is patched conics: an Earth-centered arc, a Moon-centered encounter, then an Earth-centered return. But gravity does not switch at a boundary. Precision predictions numerically integrated the equations of motion, accounting for perturbations and the changing geometry. Targeting routines iterated the maneuver until the predicted encounter satisfied its constraints. [3]

05

THE LUNAR ENCOUNTER

The Moon can turn you.
Or keep you.

Passing the Moon and orbiting it are different missions.

A / FREE RETURN

A route back.

A carefully chosen lunar flyby bends the spacecraft’s motion toward Earth without a large return maneuver. It still needs accurate targeting; navigation errors can require corrections. “Free return” is not a guarantee of safe entry.

B / LUNAR ORBIT INSERTION

A reason to brake.

To stay, Apollo fired its service propulsion engine against its motion relative to the Moon. Lunar orbit insertion reduced orbital energy enough for capture. Leaving lunar orbit later required a separate trans-Earth injection burn.

Both drawings are schematics, not integrated trajectories. Apollo missions did not all use the same profile: some later landing missions used hybrid transfers that departed a free-return path to meet landing-site requirements. [3] [4]

06

THE LAST CONSTRAINT

Home is a corridor.
Not a point.

Getting back to Earth is only part of getting back alive.

Midcourse corrections trimmed errors while they were still inexpensive to fix. A small, early velocity change can produce a large difference in arrival position after hours of flight.

The return trajectory had to meet the atmosphere with the right speed and flight-path angle. Too steep increases heating and deceleration; too shallow can cause excessive range or a skip-out. The command module also used lift and bank control to guide its atmospheric entry. [5]

Apollo 11’s reported entry conditions included an Earth-centered inertial speed of about 11.0 km/s and −6.5° at entry interface. Those are mission values, not universal safe limits. [2]
